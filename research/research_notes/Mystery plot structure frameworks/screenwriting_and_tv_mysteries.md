## 1. Core frameworks: three-act / Syd Field, Save the Cat, Story Circle, sequence approach, Kishotenketsu

### Takeaway
All the Western frameworks hang on the same skeleton. A catalyst comes at about 10%. Commitment to the new situation comes at about 20-25%. A midpoint reversal or false victory comes at 50%. An "all is lost" low comes at about 75%. Then a finale and a mirrored final image. Gulino's 8 sequences split that skeleton into roughly 10-15-minute mini-stories, each with its own partial resolution. Kishotenketsu replaces conflict with a third-part twist that reframes everything before it.

### Cited Findings
**Syd Field paradigm (three acts)**
- Plot Point 1 falls near the end of Act One, cited as between pages 20 and 30, or around page 25, in a 120-page script. — [Arc Studio: Syd Field's Paradigm](https://www.arcstudiopro.com/blog/syd-fields-paradigm); [CUNY gloss of Syd Field (PDF)](https://mes153stevenson.commons.gc.cuny.edu/files/2018/08/Three-Act-Structure.Jim-Soto-glosses-SYD_FIELD.pdf)
- The midpoint sits at about page 60 of 120, a turning point in the middle of the script that is often "a devastating reversal of the protagonist's fortune." Pinch 1 is placed halfway between Plot Point 1 and the midpoint. — [readtowrite: Pinch Point #1](https://readtowrite.substack.com/p/pinch-point-1); [sydfield.com guest blog](https://sydfield.com/guest-blog/utilizing-syd-fields-screenwriting-paradigm-to-understand-script-timeline-structureby-natalia-lazarus/)
- Field himself is quoted as saying the form matters, not the exact page numbers where Plot Points occur. — [sydfield.com guest blog](https://sydfield.com/guest-blog/utilizing-syd-fields-screenwriting-paradigm-to-understand-script-timeline-structureby-natalia-lazarus/) (secondary quote; not verified against *Screenplay*)

**Blake Snyder, Save the Cat! (2005), 15 beats on a 110-page script, with novel-adaptation percentages** — [Reedsy: Save the Cat beat sheet](https://reedsy.com/blog/guide/story-structure/save-the-cat-beat-sheet/)
| # | Beat | Page (of 110) | % | Function |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Opening Image | 1 | 1% | Snapshot of the hero's world and the tone |
| 2 | Theme Stated | 5 | 5% | The lesson is stated, often by another character |
| 3 | Set-Up | 1-10 | 1-10% | Hero, ordinary world, flaws |
| 4 | Catalyst | 12 | 10% | Inciting event |
| 5 | Debate | 12-25 | 10-20% | Hesitation |
| 6 | Break Into Two | 25 | ~20-25% | Hero commits and enters the new world |
| 7 | B Story | 30 | ~22-27% | Subplot or helper character, carries the theme |
| 8 | Fun and Games | 30-55 | 20-50% | "Promise of the premise" |
| 9 | Midpoint | 55 | 50% | False victory or false defeat, stakes raised |
| 10 | Bad Guys Close In | 55-75 | 50-75% | Pressure builds |
| 11 | All Is Lost | 75 | 75% | Rock bottom |
| 12 | Dark Night of the Soul | 75-85 | 75-80% | Reflection |

- Reedsy's own figures for Break Into Two (30% vs page 25) and B Story (22% vs page 30) disagree internally. Sudowrite independently confirms Catalyst at 10%, Midpoint at 50%, and All Is Lost at 75%. — [Reedsy](https://reedsy.com/blog/guide/story-structure/save-the-cat-beat-sheet/); [Sudowrite: What is a beat sheet](https://sudowrite.com/blog/what-is-a-beat-sheet/)

**Dan Harmon's Story Circle**
- Eight steps: You, Need, Go, Search, Find, Take, Return, Change. Harmon built it for Channel 101, the short-film festival he co-founded with Rob Schrab, so filmmakers could tell a complete story in about five minutes. The original tutorial is the Channel 101 wiki page "Story Structure 101: Super Basic Shit." — [Channel 101 Wiki (snippet only; page returned 402)](https://channel101.fandom.com/wiki/Story_Structure_101:_Super_Basic_Shit); [Kindlepreneur: Dan Harmon Story Circle](https://kindlepreneur.com/dan-harmon-story-circle/); [Wikipedia: Dan Harmon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dan_Harmon)

**Sequence approach (Frank Daniel, then Paul Gulino)**
- Gulino's *Screenwriting: The Sequence Approach* (Continuum, 2004) gives a two-hour film eight sequences: two in Act 1, four in Act 2, two in Act 3. Each sequence is "a short film which mirrors the structure of a complete film," usually 10-15 minutes long and trending shorter in recent films. Each sequence's conflict is only partly resolved, which keeps the audience engaged. — [The Story Department: The Sequence Approach](https://www.thestorydepartment.com/the-sequence-approach)
- Gulino learned the method from Frank Daniel at USC, where Daniel built the curriculum around sequences. It was once called the "Frank Daniel method." — [The Story Department](https://www.thestorydepartment.com/the-sequence-approach)
- Gulino applies it non-dogmatically: not every film he analyzes has eight sequences. One critic notes that his North by Northwest breakdown shows no low point. Analyzed films include The Graduate, Being John Malkovich, North by Northwest, and Nights of Cabiria. — [Charles Deemer review (ibiblio)](https://www.ibiblio.org/cdeemer/4books.htm); [Wikipedia: Sequence (filmmaking)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sequence_(filmmaking))

**Kishotenketsu**
- Four parts: Ki (introduction), Sho (development), Ten (twist), Ketsu (conclusion or reconciliation). It comes from Tang-dynasty Chinese four-line poetry, spread to Korea and Japan, and is common in four-panel yonkoma manga. None of the four parts requires conflict. The Ten twist can be dramatic enough to change genre, and the Ketsu unites the new viewpoint with the beginning. — [Book Riot: Kishotenketsu](https://bookriot.com/?p=475083); [StorytellingDB: Kishotenketsu](https://storytellingdb.com/kishotenketsu/); [Nelson Agency](https://nelsonagency.com/2022/01/kishotenketsu-and-non-western-story-structures/)

### Inferences
- Rough mapping to the game, as a playtime percentage of the Malibu case:
  - Opening Image / Set-Up = poster plus typewriter intro.
  - Catalyst (10%) = the macaw is missing, with Reyes at the entrance.
  - Break Into Two (~25%) = the gated entrance-to-living-room door after the bedroom. This is a literal threshold the player crosses carrying Derek's false "alive at 8" belief.
  - Midpoint (50%) = the living-room cage shatters that belief: a false-victory-to-false-defeat flip.
  - Bad Guys Close In (50-75%) = kitchen, garden, and library, where the red herrings tighten.
  - All Is Lost (~75%) = currently missing. A candidate is a moment where the leading theory (Basil, or Rosa) seems confirmed, then is undercut by Basil's garden-solve confession: he broke the latch but did not take the bird.
  - Break Into Three (80%) = pool-area photo plus schedule.
  - Finale = the final board.
  - Final Image = end-scene beat 5.
- Gulino's 8-sequence model maps almost 1:1 onto 7 rooms plus the final board. Each room is "a short film" with its own partial resolution (the local solve), which matches the design of per-room boards that unlock confrontations but never close the case.
- Harmon's circle fits the micro-loop of a single room: You (enter), Need (the board question), Go (talk and inspect), Search (topics), Find (clues), Take (solve the board, which costs a suspect their alibi), Return (confrontation topic), Change (new knowledge carried into the next room).
- Kishotenketsu suits a cozy tone. The Ten twist (the bird was gone before the 8 PM squawk; the squawk was a speaker) reframes earlier rooms without adding violence.

## 2. TV structure: teaser/cold open, act outs, five-act (and six-act) hour, tag, A/B/C plots

### Takeaway
A network hour is a teaser, then 4-6 acts, then a tag. The act-out before each commercial must hook the viewer. The Act 1 break defines the problem. The last act-out before the finale is the "all is lost" turn. Extra acts are carved from the middle, not from set-up or resolution. Streaming has loosened all of this.

### Cited Findings
- Standard hour shape: Teaser, Acts 1-4, Tag. A network may split an act to make a teaser, five acts, and a tag. One breakdown gives a 2-4-page teaser and 14-15-page acts, for 59-66 pages total. — [Movie Outline: Television Script Format](https://www.movieoutline.com/articles/television-script-format.html); [YSU: How to structure a drama (PDF)](https://fowens.people.ysu.edu/Tutorials/HowToStructureADrama.pdf)
- Act outs are the moments before commercial breaks, which showrunners use "to leave the audience surprised." — [GL Coverage: Writing act breaks for TV](https://glcoverage.com/2025/09/18/writing-act-breaks-for-tv)
- Jane Espenson (2007): "Most hour shows seem to have gone to the five-act structure now." The end of Act 1 must define the main problem: "This should still be the end of act one, certainly not any later." In four acts, the Act 3 out is the all-is-lost moment. In five acts it moves to the Act 4 out. "The new act is made out of the cloth in the middle." Early acts are usually longer than late acts. "Look at how many times it turns, and number your acts accordingly." — [Jane Espenson: The Book of Acts](https://janeespenson.com/082007/the-book-of-acts)
- Maclean's covered the move to six-act network structure and its effect on pacing. — [Maclean's: Is the six-act structure destroying network TV](https://macleans.ca/uncategorized/is-the-six-act-structure-destroying-network-tv/)
- Cable and streaming often drop act breaks, so "the act break is less important." — [Final Draft: How to structure a TV pilot](https://www.finaldraft.com/blog/how-to-structure-a-tv-pilot)

### Inferences
- The game already has natural act-outs: each room solve (fires a "X might have some explaining to do..." toast) and each room transfer (autosave). Treat every local solve as an act-out. It should end on a hook, a new question or a cornered character, never a closure. This matches the existing rule that local sentences "end on the suspicion, not the acquittal."
- Teaser = the case intro poster and typewriter page. In TV the teaser usually shows the crime or body. Here that could be one line of the squawk/vanishing at 8 PM, in the player's voice-of-record, before the poster title.
- Tag = end-scene beat 5 (comedic button; see section 6).
- A/B/C plots: A = macaw theft (timeline). B = a character thread with a thematic counterpoint (e.g., Vivienne and Derek's marriage, or Basil's wish to free the bird, which matches "freedom vs captivity"). C = runner gags (Coco's photos, Wexler's delight). A Save-the-Cat-style B story beat would introduce Basil or Petra as the "helper" whose viewpoint carries the theme.

## 3. How mysteries map onto the frameworks; inverted detective story (Columbo)

### Takeaway
Classic whodunits put the crime at the catalyst or Act 1 break and spend Act 2 on suspects and alibis. A false solution or false confession lands at the midpoint. The theory collapses or the case is complicated at the Act 2 break. The en-masse reveal is the finale. The inverted "howcatchem" (Columbo) shows the crime first, so the whole story is denouement: a cat-and-mouse contest of contradictions ending in a final gotcha.

### Cited Findings
- The inverted detective story was probably invented by R. Austin Freeman (Dr. Thorndyke, 1912), who coined "inverted detective story." Columbo popularized the "howcatchem" format, which "begins by showing the commission of the crime and its perpetrator." — [TV Tropes: Reverse Whodunnit (snippet; page 403)](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/ReverseWhoDunIt); [Wikipedia: Columbo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbo)
- Columbo format: the audience knows the killer from the opening, usually a wealthy member of high society. Columbo "rarely appears in the first act." He spots contradictions between the killer's account and the truth. Suspects underestimate him, but his persistent friendly questioning unsettles them into self-incrimination. Clues are sometimes shown beforehand but often withheld until the end. Episodes run about 70-98 minutes. — [Wikipedia: Columbo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbo)
- "Just one more thing": Columbo leaves, then turns back with a crucial question. Richard Levinson: "we had him come back and say, 'Oh, just one more thing.' It was never planned." — [Wikipedia: Columbo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbo)
- Endings: usually Columbo proves guilt through "some imaginative turnabout," and a weary, agreeable killer admits it. Some episodes end in confession or a quiet arrest, and a few twist the format ("Last Salute to the Commodore"). Critics note that "the majority of the narrative is therefore denouement." — [Encyclopedia.com: Columbo](https://www.encyclopedia.com/media/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/columbo); [Wikipedia: Columbo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbo)
- Columbo was adapted from the story "May I Come In" (published as "Dear Corpus Delicti" in Alfred Hitchcock's Mystery Magazine). — [Museum of Broadcast Communications: Columbo](https://www.museum.tv/tv-encyclopedia-4/columbo)
- A concrete three-act whodunit breakdown, Murder, She Wrote S1E20 (the bus/diner episode):
  - **Act 1:** Jessica and Sheriff Amos Tupper's car breaks down, so they take a bus. A parolee and an armed stranger (Carey) board. Plot Point 1: the bus stops at a diner and Jessica finds Gilbert Stoner dead, a screwdriver in his neck.
  - **Act 2:** Alibis are given and several are contradicted (Steve's payphone story, which Jessica saw was false). Red herrings: multiple exits, Carey's "jewelry salesman" cover. Clues: a photo, a suitcase in the rain.
  - **Midpoint, a false solution:** a clipping ties driver Ben Gibbons to the heist victim, Amos arrests him, and Gibbons confesses. Jessica proves the victim was already strangled, so she judges the confession false.
  - **Plot Point 2:** a blackout, a gunshot, the CB radio destroyed, a key book stolen.
  - **Act 3:** the book is found with a key in its binding. Jessica accuses Captain Downing (from a knot he tied). He pulls the gun, is overpowered, and admits to the search but not the murder. Climax: Jessica reveals Gibbons after all. He strangled Stoner in rage over his daughter's death, then stabbed him to disguise it.
  - **Denouement:** the storm clears, and comedic button: Amos reveals Jessica won the banquet's big-screen TV.
  — [DIY MFA: Three-Act Analysis of Murder, She Wrote](https://diymfa.com/?p=47467)
- Murder, She Wrote's formula was so rigid that one retrospective calls it "virtually identical to PERRY MASON" structurally: "the audience knew what was coming from the opening frame." Jessica arrives in town (unless she is in Cabot Cove), and the murderer unmasked is usually the least likely suspect. Earlier seasons played with the formula more than later ones. — [Goodreads author blog: Remembering Murder, She Wrote](https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/24276643-memory-lane-remembering-murder-she-wrote?tab=book); [Video Librarian review](https://videolibrarian.com/reviews/tv/murder-she-wrote-the-complete-first-season/); [All The Tropes: Murder, She Wrote](https://Allthetropes.org/wiki/Murder,_She_Wrote)

### Inferences
- Whodunit beats on the Save-the-Cat grid:
  - Crime = Catalyst (10%) or Plot Point 1 (~25%). Murder, She Wrote finds the body at the Act 1 break.
  - Suspects and alibis = Fun and Games.
  - False solution or false confession = Midpoint (the Gibbons confession).
  - Theory collapse or second incident = All Is Lost or Plot Point 2 (the blackout).
  - The overlooked clue snaps into place = Break Into Three.
  - En-masse reveal = Finale.
  - Comedic button = Final Image.
- The MSW "double bluff" (the confessed suspect is rejected, then turns out to be the killer after all) is a sophisticated near-miss pattern. The game could echo it by having a confrontation clue such as `knew_cases_empty` read first as Marsh's alibi and then as his slip.
- Columbo maps well onto the *confrontation topics*. The player (cat) is underestimated, the "just one more thing" return visit is literally the solve-gated topic, and suspects self-incriminate (Marsh blurting "the cases are EMPTY"). A cat that "leaves and comes back" fits Columbo's rhythm and is in character for a cat.
- An inverted structure is NOT recommended for the Macaw main case. The design deliberately withholds Marsh until the library. But an inverted *tutorial* or a future case could use it.

- No source documented a recurring "killer's slip" beat in Murder, She Wrote. Treat that as folklore unless verified.

## 4. Episode/film breakdowns: Knives Out, Glass Onion, Poirot, Death in Paradise, Only Murders in the Building, Monk, Psych, Sherlock/Elementary

### Takeaway
Modern mysteries stay interesting by changing *mode* at the midpoint. Knives Out turns from whodunit into Hitchcock suspense and back. Glass Onion rewinds and replays its first half from a new point of view. Formula procedurals (Death in Paradise, Monk, Murder, She Wrote) instead perfect a fixed rhythm that ends in a eureka trigger and a summation.

### Cited Findings
**Knives Out (2019)**
- Johnson's concept: "take the engine of a Hitchcock movie and put it in the middle of a whodunit." Hitchcock disliked whodunits because they rely on surprise rather than suspense. The film "begins as a traditional whodunit and orients the audience very clearly," then "turns into a Hitchcock thriller where there's a character you care about," then "turns back into a whodunit at the end." — [No Film School: Rian Johnson's writing process](https://nofilmschool.com/rian-johnson-knives-out-screenplay); [SYFY Wire](https://www.syfy.com/syfy-wire/knives-out-director-rian-johnson-explains-how-to-build-a-great-whodunnit-mystery)
- The Hitchcock turn replaces "a second act bogged down with collecting clues." — [Bruin Life: Johnson at Austin Film Festival 2025](https://bruinlife.com/rian-johnson-broke-down-how-he-subverts-the-classic-who-done-it-at-the-2025-austin-film-festival/)
- On the payoff: "the really satisfying payoff at the end is not the reveal of whodunit; it comes after that," meaning the protagonist's relationship with the family. He wanted the film "as entertaining whether you guessed it or not." — [No Film School](https://nofilmschool.com/rian-johnson-knives-out-screenplay)

**Glass Onion (2022)**
- A "fugue" structure: "split a movie exactly in half and have a revelation right in the middle that lets you repeat the first half of the movie from a different point of view." The midpoint flashback (Blanc and Helen) was the hardest scene to write. It "essentially introduces the main character" and the film's emotional stakes, giving the audience "skin in the game in the back half." The opening puzzle-box scene foreshadows the layered structure. — [Screen Rant citing Variety](https://screenrant.com/glass-onion-most-difficult-scene-why/); [TheWrap spoiler Q&A](https://www.thewrap.com/glass-onion-spoilers-rian-johnson-interview/); [ScreenCrush: How Glass Onion reveals its own ending](https://screencrush.com/how-glass-onion-reveals-its-own-ending/)
- Criticism: the long, static midpoint table conversation risks the film's pace, and some critics felt the flashback over-connects dots. — [Film Obsessive](https://filmobsessive.com/film/new-releases/glass-onion-a-knives-out-mystery-deconstructive-whodunnit-glee/); [Omaha Central Register](https://www.omahacentralregister.com/9363/arts-and-style/reviews/glass-onion-is-a-disappointing-mess)

**Poirot / Christie: the en-masse ("drawing room") denouement and the Summation Gathering**
- The en-masse denouement gathers the major suspects to summarize the case, clear up side plots, and reveal the murderer, who then confesses, is arrested, or dies. Christie used it in only 22 of 66 novels: Poirot 15 of 33, Marple 2 of 12, Tommy and Tuppence 0 of 4. In *Styles* (1920), Poirot originally solved it from a witness box. Her publisher found that unconvincing, so she moved it to the drawing room. — [CrimeReads: Kemper Donovan on Christie's best denouements](https://crimereads.com/agatha-christie-best-denouements-kemper-donovan/)
- Alternatives: *Roger Ackroyd* (an intimate one-to-one with the culprit), *Sad Cypress* (courtroom), *Death on the Nile* novel (solution told to three people, then a private confrontation). The 1978 film instead plays "what if?" vignettes imagining each suspect as killer. — [CrimeReads](https://crimereads.com/agatha-christie-best-denouements-kemper-donovan/)
- Poirot "casts suspicion on other suspects before finally nailing the culprit," and walks the suspects through his reasoning, filling gaps. — [New Savanna blog quoting Wikipedia](https://new-savanna.blogspot.com/2012/05/hercule-poirot-reveal-and-bleg.html?m=1); [LitCharts: Roger Ackroyd ch. 23](https://litcharts.com/lit/the-murder-of-roger-ackroyd/chapter-23-poirot-s-little-reunion)
- *Orient Express* structures its middle as a series of one-on-one dining-car interviews compared afterwards. — [Readify: Orient Express](https://readifyai.com/en/books/Murder%20on%20the%20Orient%20Express/wiki/Suspicion)

**Death in Paradise (BBC, 2011-)**
- Wikipedia: "formulaic approach to its plots with each episode roughly the same in length, style and narrative structure." — [Wikipedia: Death in Paradise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Death_in_Paradise)
- TV Tropes (snippet only; page returned 403): DI Poole is prone to Eureka Moments, "almost always after reading a book related to the subject of the investigation," or on finding an important clue. Goodman also has them, though not always in that pattern. — [TV Tropes: Death in Paradise](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Series/DeathInParadise)
- Critics credit the formula with the show's global success, and say it keeps "life in the formula" through small experiments. — [The Boar](https://theboar.org/2018/03/death-in-paradise-popular/); [Cultbox S6E3 review](https://cultbox.co.uk/reviews/episodes/death-in-paradise-review-season-6-episode-3)

**Only Murders in the Building (Hulu, 2021-)**
- Showrunner John Hoffman: "we have to know who killed and who, why, and how, and then backtrack and twist our way to that." He wants "a foundation of a three-act structure for a season before the writer's room opens." Season 1 had no plan for the killer at the start, while Season 2 did. Information order is the lever: "the order in which you lay out that story to get the biggest bang for your buck," aiming for "Oh, my god, I didn't see it when it was there." Tone: "humanist comedy," "the ridiculous moment meeting the truthful core of a character." They "didn't want to make a spoof." — [Script Magazine interview with John Hoffman](https://scriptmag.com/interviews-features/creating-the-murder-mystery-comedy-with-only-murders-in-the-building-co-creator-and-showrunner-john-hoffman)
- Ten-episode seasons, each ending in a cliffhanger that seeds the next season (S1 finale). The S4 premiere was "structured in a way that the audience has a sense about something that our trio doesn't" (dramatic irony). Narration bookends episodes, e.g., Cinda Canning narrating "The Sting." — [TheWrap: S1 cliffhanger](https://www.thewrap.com/only-murders-in-the-building-season-2-bunny-killer-mabel-selena-gomez-blood-knitting-needle-john-hoffman/); [TheWrap: S4 premiere](https://www.thewrap.com/only-murders-in-the-building-season-4-episode-1-john-hoffman-interview); [Hollywood Reporter](https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/tv/tv-features/only-murders-in-the-building-showrunner-interview-true-crime-satire-1235559493/)

**Monk / Psych**
- Monk: episode write-ups use a recurring "Here's what happened" summation section, the detective's walk-through before the killer is exposed. One episode stages it as a dream-sitcom with a laugh track ("Mr. Monk's Favorite Show"). — [Monk Wiki: Mr. Monk and Sharona](https://monk.fandom.com/wiki/Mr._Monk_and_Sharona); [All The Tropes: Monk](https://AllTheTropes.org/wiki/Monk)
- Psych: Shawn's fake psychic "vision" gets the police involved early, and recurs as a performative reveal device (pilot: a vision of dog-bite medication confirms his theory). Childhood flashbacks with his father Henry typically open episodes as a thematic cold open. — [Psych Wiki: Pilot](https://psychusa.fandom.com/wiki/Pilot); [TVMaze: Psych](https://www.tvmaze.com/shows/517/psych/episodeguide)

### Inferences
- **Knives Out lesson for the game:** the macaw case could deliberately switch *mode* at its midpoint (the cage room). The early rooms are a whodunit (who took it?). After the squawk is revealed as fake, the case becomes a timeline reconstruction (when?), which the final board already is. Johnson's point that the real payoff comes *after* the reveal supports a five-beat end scene that spends its weight on character resolution (Rudebeak freed or home, Basil's wish, Vivienne and Derek) rather than re-explaining.
- **Glass Onion lesson:** a midpoint "replay" fits the casebook. The notes page timeline ("The Evening") could visibly re-sort after the cage revelation, so the player sees the first half of the evening "from a different point of view."
- **Poirot lesson:** the final board is the player's own summation gathering. Christie's pattern (cast suspicion on each suspect in turn, then nail the culprit) could be staged in the end scene: beat 1 dismisses Basil (the latch), beat 2 dismisses Rosa (the cart), beat 3 turns to the Sound Guy, beat 4 is the Rudebeak reveal, and beat 5 is the button. This turns the existing near-miss decoys into the gathering's "what if?" vignettes.
- **Death in Paradise lesson:** the eureka triggered by an unrelated remark or object is a strong pattern for a cat. A non-clue flavor interaction or toast could trigger the "Break Into Three" realization. The existing intended "revisit a source to re-read a time" behavior is a player-driven eureka.
- **Only Murders lesson:** Hoffman's "know the ending, backtrack" matches how the true timeline in CLAUDE.md is authored. "I didn't see it when it was there" is the target feeling for clues like the straw hat at the photo's edge.
- **Monk/Psych:** a comedic, performative summation suits the cozy tone. The cat's "summation" could be wordless actions (knocking items off a table in order), in keeping with "the cat cannot talk."

- **Sherlock (BBC) and Elementary:** no sources retrieved in this session (tool budget). Not covered here, including Sherlock's on-screen text "mind palace" deduction visualization and Elementary's procedural cold opens.

## 5. Scene-level craft: enter late/leave early, scene turns a value, scene goals, reveals at act breaks

### Takeaway
McKee's test is whether a scene turns a value-charged condition (positive to negative or back). If nothing changes, nothing happened, and the scene is exposition. Vary the direction of the turns. Hold the biggest reveals for the act-outs.

### Cited Findings
- McKee: round every scene "by turning a value at stake in a character's life from the positive to the negative or the negative to the positive." Check: what value is at stake at the open, how is it charged, and "Where is this value now?" at the close. — [McKee Story: Do your scenes turn?](https://mckeestory.com/do-your-scenes-turn/)
- "Every true scene turns the value-charged condition of the character's life," and the degree of change varies by event. If the start charge does not change, the scene is flat, probably exposition. — [Michelle Styles: story values (McKee reprise)](https://michellestyles.blogspot.com/2007/11/story-values-mckee-reprise.html?m=1); [Stage 32: Scene charges (Frankie Gaddo)](https://stage32.com/post/2208727289561096655)
- Vary the shift patterns (negative to more negative, positive to negative to positive). Avoid three consecutive scenes with the same positive-to-negative shift. — [Jennifer Ellis: Scene turns or polarity shifts](https://jennifer-ellis.squarespace.com/blog/2016/3/10/0lh89i31y69nzn0jcsk3arrv6kao62)
- Act outs (the moment before an ad break) are where TV places surprises and reveals. — [GL Coverage](https://glcoverage.com/2025/09/18/writing-act-breaks-for-tv); [Jane Espenson](https://janeespenson.com/082007/the-book-of-acts)

### Inferences
- In the game, each interactable conversation is a "scene," and each should turn a value. Some examples:
  - Trust: Reyes goes from dismissive to cooperating.
  - Certainty: Derek goes from sure to doubting.
  - Suspicion: Rosa goes from clear to suspect.
  Flavor objects that turn nothing are the "empty interactions" playtesters disliked.
- "Enter late, leave early": character intros should open mid-action ("mid-affirmation" Vivienne), and topics should end on the line that carries the slip, not after it. That fits the existing 2-3-sentence-per-page rule.
- Use the per-room solve as the act-out reveal: the confrontation unlocked by the solve should flip a value (an alibi cracked) and end on an open question.

### Gaps
- "Enter late, leave early" is a common maxim often attributed to William Goldman or David Mamet. No authoritative source was retrieved this session.
- McKee's beat / scene / sequence / act hierarchy definitions were not fetched verbatim.

## 6. Epilogue / denouement: length and function

### Takeaway
Mystery denouements do three jobs. They explain or confirm the solution, either en masse or privately. They dispatch the culprit (confession, arrest, or death). They close character arcs with a lighthearted button. Johnson argues the real emotional payoff comes after the whodunit reveal.

### Cited Findings
- Murder, She Wrote S1E20 denouement is very short: the storm clears, the killer is taken away, the journey resumes, and a comedic button (Amos reveals Jessica won the TV). Described as a typical lighthearted closing beat. — [DIY MFA](https://diymfa.com/?p=47467)
- Christie denouements: summarize the case, clear side plots, reveal the murderer, then confession, arrest, or death. Format varies by story. — [CrimeReads](https://crimereads.com/agatha-christie-best-denouements-kemper-donovan/)
- Columbo: the gotcha usually closes the episode, often with the killer's weary admission. The bulk of the episode is effectively denouement. — [Wikipedia: Columbo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbo); [Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/media/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/columbo)
- Knives Out: "the really satisfying payoff at the end is not the reveal of whodunit; it comes after that" (Marta's relationship to the family, the final image). — [No Film School](https://nofilmschool.com/rian-johnson-knives-out-screenplay)
- Save the Cat Final Image (99-100%) mirrors the Opening Image to show change. — [Reedsy](https://reedsy.com/blog/guide/story-structure/save-the-cat-beat-sheet/)

### Inferences
- A five-beat end scene template:
  1. Gathering and dismissal of red herrings.
  2. Culprit's motive confirmed (47 takes).
  3. Culprit's dispatch (Reyes).
  4. Rudebeak reveal (emotional payoff, per Johnson).
  5. Comedic button that mirrors the poster or opening line (Final Image).
- Keep it to roughly 5-10% of total case playtime. TV tags run a page or two, about 1-2 minutes of a 45-minute hour.

### Gaps
- No quantitative source on denouement length as a percentage of runtime. The 5-10% figure above is an inference from beat-sheet percentages (Finale 80-99%, Final Image 99-100%).
