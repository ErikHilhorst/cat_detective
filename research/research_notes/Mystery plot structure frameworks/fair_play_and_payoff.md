# Fair-Play Mystery Rules, Reveal Satisfaction, Red Herrings, and Setup/Payoff

Scope: craft theory from written (and some film) mystery fiction, turned into heuristics for "Cat Detective - The Missing Macaw" (culprit: D. Marsh, the sound guy, who faked the 8 PM squawk with a hidden speaker; red herrings: Basil the gardener, who broke the latch; Rosa the maid; Derek the husband).

Sourcing note: about 15 tool calls. Primary texts were reached for Knox (via Wikipedia's quotations) and Van Dine (full reprint). Chandler's "Casual Notes" is cited only through a quotation aggregator and archive records. Several craft claims come from secondary blogs and are marked as such.

## 1. Knox's Ten Commandments and Van Dine's Twenty Rules: what they say, and what still applies

### Takeaway
Both codes come down to one rule: the reader must have a real, equal chance to solve the case from clues shown on the page, and the culprit must be a known, early-introduced character exposed by deduction, not luck. Rules about romance, servants, gimmick bans, and "there must be a corpse" are period taste and are now routinely broken, especially in cozies.

### Cited Findings
**Knox's Decalogue (1929):**
- (1) "The criminal must be mentioned in the early part of the story," and must not be someone whose thoughts the reader has been allowed to follow. (2) No supernatural agencies. (3) No more than one secret room or passage. (4) No undiscovered poisons, and no device "which will need a long scientific explanation at the end." (5) "No Chinaman": an openly racist rule of its time. (6) "No accident must ever help the detective, nor must he ever have an unaccountable intuition which proves to be right." (7) The detective must not commit the crime. (8) "The detective is bound to declare any clues which he may discover." (9) The Watson's intelligence should be "slightly, but very slightly, below that of the average reader." (10) Twins and doubles are banned "unless we have been duly prepared for them." — [Wikipedia: Ronald Knox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Knox)

**Van Dine's "Twenty Rules for Writing Detective Stories" (*The American Magazine*, Sept. 1928)**, paraphrased from the full reprint — [UMSL reprint](https://www.umsl.edu/~gradyf/film/vandinerules.htm):
- R1: the reader gets "equal opportunity with the detective," and all clues are plainly stated and described.
- R2: "No wilful tricks or deceptions may be played on the reader" other than those the criminal plays on the detective.
- R3: no love interest.
- R4: the detective or an official investigator is never the culprit.
- R5: the culprit is found by logical deduction, "not by accident or coincidence or unmotivated confession."
- R6: "a detective is not a detective unless he detects."
- R7: there must be a corpse.
- R8: naturalistic means only.
- R9: one detective.
- R10: the culprit is "a person with whom the reader is familiar and in whom he takes an interest."
- R11: servants should not be the culprit (too easy).
- R12: one culprit; minor accomplices are allowed.
- R13: no secret societies or mafias.
- R14: no pseudo-science.
- R15: the truth must be discoverable. Looking back, the clues point to the culprit, "provided the reader is shrewd enough to see it."
- R16: no long descriptive passages.
- R17: no professional criminals as the guilty party.
- R18: the death is never an accident or suicide.
- R19: personal motives.
- R20: banned gimmicks: the cigarette-butt clue, the fake seance, forged fingerprints, the dummy-figure alibi, the dog that does not bark, the twin culprit, the hypodermic or knockout drops, the locked room the police break into, the word-association test, and the cipher.
- A 2025 CrimeReads piece restates the modern core: the clues shown must be the clues the detective actually uses; a motive is required, however flimsy; the culprit must be an identified suspect, not "a minor figure who appears briefly and vanishes"; red herrings and lying suspects are allowed; and the detective may keep his working theory to himself. — [Hazell Ward, CrimeReads](https://crimereads.com/a-magicians-contract-between-reader-and-writer-rules-for-delivering-a-whodunit/)
- A 2023 study of Christie by Sally and Tony Hope opens with Christie as a "rule breaker/bender" of the Knox Decalogue. — [Cross-Examining Crime review](https://crossexaminingcrime.com/2024/01/06/agatha-christie-plots-clues-and-misdirections-thirty-three-ways-the-queen-of-crime-deceives-us-2023-by-sally-and-tony-hope/)
- Cozies routinely include "a possible love interest (but no sex scenes)," which directly breaks Van Dine R3. — [Novel Suspects](https://novelsuspects.com/articles/what-makes-a-cozy-mystery) / search summary of cozy-genre guides

### Inferences
**Still matter (apply to Cat Detective):**
- Knox 1 and Van Dine R10: the culprit is introduced early. The game deliberately delays Marsh's *name* until the library, but he must still be physically present early. The Sound Guy appears without a name and is first heard of through the cable and the speaker. Keep him as a present, interactable character early on, not a name that turns up in room 6.
- Knox 8 and Van Dine R1/R2: every clue the final board needs must be a clue the player can actually collect. The game's ungated-source rule for answers already enforces this.
- Knox 6 and Van Dine R5/R6: no accidental solve and no unmotivated confession. Confrontations as "slips, never solutions" fits this exactly.
- Knox 4: no solution that needs a long technical lecture. The speaker-and-cable trick is simple enough to explain in a single beat.
- R15, the hindsight test: after the end scene, a player mentally replaying the 6:30 registry entry, the 6:47 photo, the 7:00 manifest, and the cable should see that it was all there.
- R19, a personal motive: 47 ruined takes is personal, petty, and very cozy.

**Outdated or safe to break:**
- R3 (no romance), R7 (there must be a corpse: a missing macaw is fine in a cozy), R9 (one detective), R16 (no description: atmosphere is part of a cozy game's appeal), and R11 (no servant culprit: as a *convention*, it is what makes Rosa an effective red herring, since players have absorbed "the butler did it").
- Knox 5 is racist and should simply be ignored.

**Translate to games:** Knox 9 (the Watson slightly dimmer than the reader) maps to tone. The narrator or toasts should never be ahead of the player. The project's rule that the narrator never explains what a slip means already does this.

### Gaps
- The verbatim primary text of Knox's 1929 preface to *The Best Detective Stories of 1928-29* was not fetched. The quotations come via Wikipedia.

## 2. What makes a reveal satisfying

### Takeaway
The solution must feel *inevitable in hindsight* (Chandler) while being surprising in the moment. This works only if every link was on the page. The best modern practice (Rian Johnson) adds a second "engine" besides the end twist, such as suspense or early partial reveals, and then pays off with a gathering-style explanation that walks back through the clues.

### Cited Findings
- Chandler: "The solution, once revealed, must seem to have been inevitable." He adds that at least half of all mystery novels break this rule. From "Casual Notes on the Mystery Novel" (1949), first published in *Raymond Chandler Speaking* (1962). — [Quotepark](https://quotepark.com/quotes/1747162-raymond-chandler-the-solution-once-revealed-must-seem-to-have-bee/); the essay also exists as a typescript, "Twelve Notes on the Mystery Story" — [Bodleian archive](https://archives.bodleian.ox.ac.uk/repositories/2/archival_objects/693671)
- Van Dine R15: looking back, the reader should see that the clues pointed to the culprit. — [UMSL reprint](https://www.umsl.edu/~gradyf/film/vandinerules.htm)
- Ward's magician analogy: a detective who shows his empty sleeves before producing the rabbit impresses; one who skips that step is "just a man with a rabbit in his hat." Readers expect to be fooled, but they do not want to solve the case in the first thirty pages, because an easy solve makes the detective seem smaller. — [CrimeReads](https://crimereads.com/a-magicians-contract-between-reader-and-writer-rules-for-delivering-a-whodunit/)
- Ellery Queen's "Challenge to the Reader" was a single page near the end where the story stopped and told the reader they now had every clue Ellery had and only one solution was possible. It was a deliberate demonstration of fair play. Critics note it sometimes made the puzzle too easy for experienced readers. — [Wikipedia mirror: Ellery Queen](https://en.wikipedia.com/wiki/Manfred_Lee); [Aunt Agatha's](https://auntagathas.com/aa/?p=7014); see also [CrimeReads: authors reviving fair-play puzzles](https://crimereads.com/fair-play-puzzle-mysteries/)
- Rian Johnson: the whodunit "usually hinges on surprise and not suspense," and he agrees with Hitchcock that suspense is "a much stronger engine." Rather than making "this person was lying the whole time!" the final twist, he shows the truth early so viewers ask "Why did that person lie in this way?", which he found "more fruitful." — [/Film interview](https://www.slashfilm.com/570686/rian-johnson-interview-knives-out/)
- Johnson warns against "one big buildup to a cheap surprise at the end": "It's not just clue-gathering. There's something else going on." He recalls Truman Capote's complaint about detective fiction: "You withheld information that was essential to solving it until the very end!" — [SYFY Wire](https://www.syfy.com/syfy-wire/knives-out-director-rian-johnson-explains-how-to-build-a-great-whodunnit-mystery)
- Johnson describes Hitchcock's bomb under the table: if the bomb simply goes off, the audience gets one moment of surprise; if it is shown first, they feel suspense for the whole scene. — [PBS: conversation with Rian Johnson](https://www.pbs.org/video/reinventing-the-classic-whodunnit-a-conversation-with-rian-johnson-7vtzs4/)
- Johnson on the denouement: *Knives Out* was "predicated on having that scene at the end," and he calls the final explanation, with its flashbacks, "like candy." He names "a pleasing moral certitude" as a core genre pleasure: the good detective solves it and the guilty are punished. — [/Film](https://www.slashfilm.com/570686/rian-johnson-interview-knives-out/)
- His founding concept was to hide a whodunit inside a Hitchcock thriller: "to get everything I love about whodunits and put the engine of the [Alfred] Hitchcock thriller in there." — [Columbia Chronicle](https://columbiachronicle.com/campus/director-rian-johnson-talks-knives-out-exploring-genres-and-hitchcock-at-columbia-film-row/)
- Horowitz on *Magpie Murders*: he aimed for a book that "wouldn't be confusing to readers and would provide satisfaction at the end but which in itself is incredibly intricate," and kept a notebook of clues and red herrings. — search summary of [Publishers Weekly profile](https://publishersweekly.com/pw/print/20170619/74005-and-then-there-was-horowitz.html). This was not fetched directly, so treat the wording as approximate.
- P.D. James calls the detective story "a small celebration of reason and order in our very disorderly world." — search summary of [NPR on *Talking About Detective Fiction*](https://www.npr.org/2009/12/22/121721631/p-d-james-talking-and-writing-detective-fiction)

### Inferences
- **The final board is the game's Challenge to the Reader.** The "M/N confronted" lock plays the role of Queen's declaration: you now hold everything. The board itself is the "gathering of suspects." The end scene should do the *candy* job: replay the key clues in order (6:30 registry, 6:47 photo, 7:00 manifest, cable to the speaker, the 47 takes) as quick flashback beats, so the player gets the hindsight rush instead of only a confession.
- **Surprise vs. suspense in this case:** the Derek-to-cage sequence is a suspense device. The player believes the 8 PM anchor and then watches it break at the cage. That is Johnson's "show something early, ask why" in practice. More of the same: mid-game reveals that change the question (the squawk was fake, so *who* faked it?) keep the player moving without waiting on one final twist.
- **The inevitability test for the designer:** list every inference link from the opening to "Marsh did it." Each link should have at least one player-visible source, and none should depend on knowledge the player never got (Capote's complaint).
- **Do not over-signal.** Queen's challenge sometimes made solves too easy. Keep the confrontation lock's toasts neutral, as the project already does.

### Gaps
- No reliable source was found that lays out Christie's "gathering of suspects" denouement as a theory, though the convention is well attested in general knowledge. It needs a dedicated source if it is cited.
- The rest of Chandler's twelve notes, and the specific arguments of "The Simple Art of Murder" (1944, his attack on Golden Age artificiality), were not retrieved. See [Wikipedia: The Simple Art of Murder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Simple_Art_of_Murder) as a starting point.

## 3. Misdirection techniques (Christie and others)

### Takeaway
Misdirection works through *attention*, not omission. Hide the key clue among more memorable objects, follow it with something more exciting, or split it in half and separate the halves. Christie's recurring tools include identity deception, a worthless-looking item that turns out to matter, a hidden intended victim, and unlikely partners.

### Cited Findings
- Three core methods. **Concealment**: hide the key clue among innocent but more memorable objects (the list trick). **Misdirection**: focus the reader on the wrong thing, or follow a clue with a more exciting event so it is forgotten. **Delay**: present the clue late, or present it early and attach its meaning much later, once the reader has forgotten it. Disguised clues still count as fair. — [CrimeReads, Ward](https://crimereads.com/a-magicians-contract-between-reader-and-writer-rules-for-delivering-a-whodunit/)
- Techniques catalogued by the Hopes:
  - "Plenty of Nothing": "What appears to be valueless might be priceless."
  - "Sawing the clue in half": the two halves of one clue placed in different parts of the book.
  - Clue clusters.
  - Concealing the intended victim ("Missing the Target").
  - "Unlikely partners in crime."
  - Identity theft: "People may not be who they seem to be," present in about 60% of Christie's novels.

  Source: [Cross-Examining Crime review of *Agatha Christie: Plots, Clues and Misdirections* (2023)](https://crossexaminingcrime.com/2024/01/06/agatha-christie-plots-clues-and-misdirections-thirty-three-ways-the-queen-of-crime-deceives-us-2023-by-sally-and-tony-hope/)
- Golden Age authors played "a game of bluff and double-bluff" with the reader's knowledge of genre conventions. — search summary of [K. Seago, "Red Herrings and other misdirection in translation" (City, Univ. of London)](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/3852/1/K%20Seago%20Red%20Herrings%20and%20other%20misdirection%20in%20translation.pdf)
- In *The Murder of Roger Ackroyd* the clues are "hidden in plain sight." The unreliable narrator split critics over whether Christie was a master manipulator or "simply cheat[ed]." — [inkl / Guardian, Sam Jordison](https://www.inkl.com/news/how-agatha-christie-hides-her-plot-secrets-in-plain-sight); [CrimeReads](https://crimereads.com/a-magicians-contract-between-reader-and-writer-rules-for-delivering-a-whodunit/)

### Inferences (applied)
- **Hiding in lists works directly in the game:** the registry and the manifest, with chaff entries like "Linen return - 7:10 PM," already do this. Keep the key entry formatted exactly like the noise entries.
- **Sawing the clue in half is the timeline mechanic:** event chips and time chips are separate, and the player re-pairs them (the 7:00 call on the pool schedule against the 6:30 registry entry). That is a textbook Christie split.
- **"Follow it with something more exciting":** put the cable clue in the same room as a loud distraction (the glove, the polish cloth, the broken latch) so it is fair but easy to pass over.
- **The double bluff and the obvious suspect:** Basil is the "obvious" suspect who confesses to the latch. His confession should feel like it *almost* closes the case, so that the second half (what he witnessed behind the shed) pushes the player on to Marsh.
- **"Plenty of nothing":** the sound guy *stopping* complaining (Wexler) is a negative clue, the absence of an expected behaviour. Absence clues are elegant and fair.

- The full list of the Hopes' 33 techniques was not available. The review lists only some chapter headings.

## 4. Red herrings: building them, resolving them, not cheating

### Takeaway
A good red herring is a *real character with a real secret* whose suspicious behaviour has an honest explanation that is revealed later. It should "play off" the true solution rather than being random noise, and its exposure should pay off as a subplot or a character beat.

### Cited Findings
- "Give every one of your significant characters a secret." Mix shocking and private secrets, know the motive behind each, use only the strongest in the plot, resolve each planted herring through confession, motive, or alibi, and track secrets in a spreadsheet (herring or backstory, resolved or not). Don't over-explain: an immaterial herring in a brisk plot may need no explanation. — [Kris Calvin, Writer's Digest (2021)](https://writersdigest.com/write-better-fiction/planting-clues-red-herrings-that-fool-but-dont-frustrate-your-readers)
- A suspect's secret answers the question: "what reason might this character have to lie to, evade, or otherwise refuse to cooperate with the protagonist?" — [Killer Nashville](https://www.killernashville.com/articles/the-writers-life-suspects-secrets-and-sleight-of-hand)
- Spend time with *all* the suspects so the red herrings don't stand out. — [Sally Carpenter, Goodreads blog](https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/2255172-toss-in-those-red-herrings-by-sally-carpenter?tab=book) (via search snippet)
- Every red herring should play off the real answer; when it is exposed, the reader should get a satisfying reason for the misleading behaviour; it should be believable from credible evidence, not deliberate deception of the reader. — [Story Grid: Red Herrings](https://storygrid.com/red-herrings/); [Elaine Orr](https://elaineorr.blogspot.com/2018/11/staying-true-to-your-mysterys-red.html) (via search snippets)
- Classic examples: "Rache" written at the scene in *A Study in Scarlet* sends police toward a German suspect, and Bishop Aringarosa in *The Da Vinci Code* turns out to have been manipulated. — [Wikipedia: Red herring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_herring). Norman Bates's "mother" seen in the window in *Psycho*. — [Jericho Writers](https://jerichowriters.com/what-is-chekhovs-gun/)
- A red herring is the legitimate exception to Chekhov's gun: a planted element that pays off *misleadingly*. — [Jericho Writers](https://jerichowriters.com/what-is-chekhovs-gun/)
- Van Dine R2 permits only the deceptions the criminal plays on the detective, which is why a herring must come from in-world behaviour, not narrator trickery. — [UMSL reprint](https://www.umsl.edu/~gradyf/film/vandinerules.htm)

### Inferences (applied to the Macaw cast)
- **Basil**'s true secret is that he broke the latch to free the bird. He resolves as a sympathetic subplot (animal-lover guilt) and becomes the *witness* to the real crime. This is the gold standard: the herring's truth hands over a real clue.
- **Rosa**'s secret should be something personal and benign (the motive note, the polish cloth). Make sure her resolution is stated by the end scene, because the cozy "everything clarified" ending requires it.
- **Derek** is honestly wrong, not lying. A herring built on sincere false belief (the 8 PM squawk through his headphones) is fair because the in-world deception (Marsh's speaker) fooled him too.
- **Chip and Wexler** are lighter, comedic herrings. Calvin's "don't over-explain" applies, and a one-line resolution is enough.
- **Herring checklist:** (a) what is the secret? (b) why do they hide it? (c) which clue makes them look guilty? (d) which clue or confrontation clears or explains them? (e) does their truth contribute a real clue to the main solution?

### Gaps
- No academic source was found on "herrings resolving into subplots" as a named technique. This rests on practitioner blogs.

## 5. Setup/payoff, foreshadowing, and fair concealment of the key clue

### Takeaway
Every memorable element must earn its place (Chekhov), but in mysteries the payoff is often *reinterpretation*: the clue appears early in an innocent context and its meaning arrives late ("plant early, pay late"). Concealment is fair only if the clue is perceivable. Disguise is allowed; omission is not.

### Cited Findings
- Chekhov's principle, usually traced to an 1889 letter to Aleksandr Lazarev: "One must never place a loaded rifle on the stage if it isn't going to go off. It's wrong to make promises you don't mean to keep." The exact wording varies by source and its origin is "a bit sketchy." — [Final Draft](https://www.finaldraft.com/blog/what-is-chekhovs-gun); [Wikipedia (archived)](https://webcf.waybackmachine.org/web/20220323202537/https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chekhov%27s_gun)
- Chekhov's gun differs from foreshadowing: the "gun" directly affects the plot, while foreshadowing only hints. The Othello handkerchief is the standard plant-and-payoff example. — [Jericho Writers](https://jerichowriters.com/what-is-chekhovs-gun/)
- Delay technique: give the clue early and attach its meaning only later, once the reader has forgotten it. — [CrimeReads](https://crimereads.com/a-magicians-contract-between-reader-and-writer-rules-for-delivering-a-whodunit/)
- Knox 10, "duly prepared for": twins and doubles are allowed only if set up in advance. In general, any surprising mechanism must be planted. — [Wikipedia: Ronald Knox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Knox)
- Don't break the story's established rules (no clones or disguise potions unless the world allows them). — [Writer's Digest](https://writersdigest.com/write-better-fiction/planting-clues-red-herrings-that-fool-but-dont-frustrate-your-readers)

### Inferences (applied)
- **Plant the mechanism of the trick early:** the player should meet audio gear and "speakers can fake sounds" in an innocent context *before* the hidden speaker is found. Examples: Derek's spare audio cable in the bedroom, or film-set sound gear visible in the entrance. That is Knox 10's "duly prepared," applied to the speaker.
- **Plant the motive early, pay it late:** the ruined takes could appear as background grumbling (Wexler, crew chatter) before the session log makes them concrete.
- **Every flavour object pays something** (Chekhov). This matches the project's playtest finding that empty interactions frustrated players.
- **Fair concealment test:** could a player who reads every dialogue page, without guessing, reconstruct the 6:30 / 7:00 half-hour gap? If yes, the clue is fair however buried it is.

### Gaps
- No sourced text was found for the specific phrase "plant early, pay late." It is a common craft idiom, but the attribution could not be verified.

## 6. Cozy mystery conventions

### Takeaway
A cozy is an amateur sleuth in a small, socially intimate community, with violence offstage, likable characters, humour, many red herrings, sidelined police, and a closing restoration of order in which justice prevails and every thread is clarified.

### Cited Findings
- Markers of a cozy: small-town setting, amateur sleuth, likable characters, murder in the background with no gory description, a possible love interest without sex scenes, "loads of red herrings," and a sense of humour. Sex and violence happen offstage, and the detection takes place in "a small, socially intimate community." — search summaries of [Novel Suspects](https://novelsuspects.com/articles/what-makes-a-cozy-mystery), [HuffPost](https://www.huffpost.com/entry/what-defines-a-cozy-mystery_b_5995b2f9e4b055243ea13675), and [TV Tropes: Cozy Mystery](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/CozyMystery)
- Malice Domestic defines traditional mysteries as containing "no explicit sex or excessive gore or violence." Police are often "uninterested in what the amateur has to offer." — search summary of [Barton CCC library cozy guide](https://docs.bartonccc.edu/stuservices/library/fiction%20genres/cozy-mysteries.pdf) / [Novel Suspects](https://novelsuspects.com/articles/what-makes-a-cozy-mystery)
- "In the end, justice always prevails and the sleuth is the heroine (or hero)." The ending "must clarify all parts of the mystery," because it is a commercial ending, and both sleuth and reader must feel satisfied. — search summaries of [Writers Write: How to write a cozy mystery](https://www.writerswrite.co.za/?p=1265040) and [phdiva blog](https://phdiva.blog/2023/08/14/cozy-mysteries-101-what-defines-the-genre)
- Johnson's "pleasing moral certitude": the good detective solves it and the guilty party is punished. — [/Film](https://www.slashfilm.com/570686/rian-johnson-interview-knives-out/)
- P.D. James's "small celebration of reason and order." — [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2009/12/22/121721631/p-d-james-talking-and-writing-detective-fiction)

### Inferences (applied)
- **Fits the game:** a cat as the ultimate amateur (police literally ignore him, as Reyes's "whiskers" lines show), a closed mansion community, no corpse, comedic suspects (Chip's PR panic, Wexler's delight in drama), and an animal victim who is *recovered*, not killed.
- **Restoration beat:** the end scene should show order restored *and* each herring's fate: Basil forgiven or redeemed, Rosa cleared, Rudebeak home. The cozy contract demands that every thread be clarified, which is stricter than Calvin's "don't over-explain" for general mysteries. For the main cast, clarify.
- **Proportionate justice:** the culprit's motive is petty (ruined takes), so the comeuppance should be gentle and comedic rather than grim.

### Gaps
- Sources are mostly genre blogs and library guides. No scholarly treatment of cozy conventions was retrieved.

## 7. Common failure modes

### Takeaway
Most failures break the fair-play contract: a culprit the reader barely met, deduction that depends on unseen information, a solve that comes by luck or confession, or the opposite problem, a solution so obvious the detective looks foolish.

### Cited Findings
- **Late or minor culprit:** a "minor figure who appears briefly and vanishes" is not a fair solution — [CrimeReads](https://crimereads.com/a-magicians-contract-between-reader-and-writer-rules-for-delivering-a-whodunit/). "Play fair with the killer's introduction": don't reveal the murderer late, after the clues have pointed elsewhere — [Writer's Digest](https://writersdigest.com/write-better-fiction/planting-clues-red-herrings-that-fool-but-dont-frustrate-your-readers). See also Knox 1 and Van Dine R10.
- **Withheld information:** Capote's complaint that essential information was "withheld until the very end" — [SYFY Wire](https://www.syfy.com/syfy-wire/knives-out-director-rian-johnson-explains-how-to-build-a-great-whodunnit-mystery). One critic faulted Horowitz's *The Word is Murder* for a solution that depended on information the reader never had, with more red herrings than useful clues — search summary of [lofaber substack review](https://lofaber.substack.com/p/the-word-is-murder-anthony-horowitz).
- **Too easy:** solving it in the first thirty pages deflates the detective — [CrimeReads](https://crimereads.com/a-magicians-contract-between-reader-and-writer-rules-for-delivering-a-whodunit/). Make the reader's quest "not too easy" — [Writer's Digest](https://writersdigest.com/write-better-fiction/planting-clues-red-herrings-that-fool-but-dont-frustrate-your-readers). Queen's challenges sometimes overdid fairness — [Aunt Agatha's](https://auntagathas.com/aa/?p=7014).
- **Luck, intuition, or confession:** Knox 6 and Van Dine R5 — [Wikipedia: Knox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Knox); [UMSL](https://www.umsl.edu/~gradyf/film/vandinerules.htm).
- **A rule-breaking or unprepared solution:** clones or disguise potions — [Writer's Digest](https://writersdigest.com/write-better-fiction/planting-clues-red-herrings-that-fool-but-dont-frustrate-your-readers). Knox 4 and Knox 10.
- **A cheap twist ending:** "one big buildup to a cheap surprise at the end" — [SYFY Wire](https://www.syfy.com/syfy-wire/knives-out-director-rian-johnson-explains-how-to-build-a-great-whodunnit-mystery).
- **No motive:** a random-victim killer is not welcome — [CrimeReads](https://crimereads.com/a-magicians-contract-between-reader-and-writer-rules-for-delivering-a-whodunit/).
- **A bloated ending:** tying up every loop — [Writer's Digest](https://writersdigest.com/write-better-fiction/planting-clues-red-herrings-that-fool-but-dont-frustrate-your-readers).

### Inferences (do/don't checklist for the designer)
- DO make Marsh a talkable presence from the first third (even unnamed). DON'T let room 6 be his first appearance.
- DO check that every final-board answer has a source the player can see. DON'T let the solve depend on outside knowledge (for example, how speakers work) unless it is planted.
- DO make confrontations slips, so the player's board is the deduction. DON'T let any NPC say "Marsh did it."
- DO give at least two real near-miss suspects at the final board. DON'T leave Marsh as the only character with evidence attached, or the solve becomes too easy.
- DO make the "idiot plot" impossible: give a reason the household hasn't solved it themselves (the police are distracted, everyone heard the fake squawk and so trusts the 8 PM anchor). The idiot-plot point itself was not directly sourced this session.
- DON'T end on a plain confession card. Walk back through the clues.

### Gaps
- No dedicated source on the "idiot plot" (a plot that runs only because characters behave stupidly) was retrieved. The term is commonly attributed to Roger Ebert or James Blish, but that was not verified here.

## 8. Craft writers: positions in brief

### Takeaway
The canon is consistent. Sayers and the Golden Age treat fair play as paramount; Chandler demands inevitability; James calls the genre a restoration of order; Horowitz aims for an intricate machine that never confuses; Johnson wants a second engine (suspense) plus an end explanation that is "candy."

### Cited Findings
- Sayers treated fair play as the most important element of detective fiction, analysing Poe's stories in those terms. — search summary of [Wheaton journal *VII*: "Edgar Allan Poe, Dorothy L. Sayers, and Detective Fiction"](https://journals.wheaton.edu/index.php/vii/article/view/989)
- P.D. James inherited the fair-play convention that the sleuth must not know more than the reader, wrote more warmly of Sayers, Allingham and Marsh than of Christie, and called the genre a "celebration of reason and order." — search summaries of [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2009/12/22/121721631/p-d-james-talking-and-writing-detective-fiction) and [Globe and Mail review](https://www.theglobeandmail.com/arts/books-and-media/review-talking-about-detective-fiction-by-pd-james/article4302445/). One critic disputes her claim that the genre lacked intellectual respect before *Gaudy Night* — [At the Scene of the Crime blog](https://at-scene-of-crime.blogspot.com/2011/10/talking-about-talking-about-detective.html).
- Chandler: the solution must seem inevitable once revealed. — [Quotepark](https://quotepark.com/quotes/1747162-raymond-chandler-the-solution-once-revealed-must-seem-to-have-bee/)
- Horowitz: intricate but not confusing, satisfying at the end. — [Publishers Weekly](https://publishersweekly.com/pw/print/20170619/74005-and-then-there-was-horowitz.html) (via search summary)
- Johnson: suspense over surprise, early truths that raise "why did they lie?", a gathering scene "like candy," and moral certitude. — [/Film](https://www.slashfilm.com/570686/rian-johnson-interview-knives-out/); [PBS](https://www.pbs.org/video/reinventing-the-classic-whodunnit-a-conversation-with-rian-johnson-7vtzs4/)
- Johnson deliberately rebuilt Christie's stock cast ("the grumpy old colonel and the maid and the butler") for 2019 without parody. — [/Film](https://www.slashfilm.com/570686/rian-johnson-interview-knives-out/)

### Inferences
- The Macaw cast (star, husband, manager, maid, gardener, director, socialite) is a Johnson-style rebuild of the Christie stock cast for Malibu. Lean into the archetypes for instant readability, but give each one a real secret (section 4).

### Gaps
- **Julian Symons** (*Bloody Murder*, 1972) was not researched in the time available. His argument that the puzzle novel gave way to the "crime novel" of character and psychology would need a source.
- **Chandler's "The Simple Art of Murder"** and the full **"Twelve Notes"** were not fetched. Only the inevitability quote is sourced.
- **Sayers**'s own introduction to *The Omnibus of Crime* (1928) was not retrieved directly.
- Horowitz's NPR interview (2017) timed out. [NPR link](https://www.npr.org/2017/06/13/532173809/what-makes-a-good-whodunit-magpie-murders-author-spells-it-out) is worth a retry.
