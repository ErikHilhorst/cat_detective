# Make every room move the case

Craft traditions from novels, screenwriting, tabletop RPGs, boxed deduction games and deduction video games agree on one structural rule that Cat Detective only half follows: **every unit at every scale must turn something, end only partly resolved, and feed the unit above it**. "The Missing Macaw" already does the micro layer well: one fact per chip, guaranteed delivery through `[keywords]`, ungated answers. It also does the endgame well, with a timeline board, a confrontation lock and an end scene. The weak joint is the meso layer. The in-house readout (`research/structure_analysis_framework.md`) finds that only **3 of 7 room boards rehearse a final-board answer** and the kitchen feeds the final board nothing. MicroMacro: Crime City, Golden Idol and Gulino's sequence approach all show what that costs: when sub-questions don't build toward the macro answer, the local solves feel like chores. The second gap is the case's best deduction. The bird left at about 6:00, so the 8 PM squawk was staged, but no board ever asks for it. That turns the Big Lie into background instead of a payoff. The research also supports four smaller changes. The final seven-slot board should give coarser feedback, because an exact "N/7 fit" invites chip-by-chip brute force. The game needs an opt-in, in-fiction hint ladder. The culprit should be talkable early, even unnamed. The five-beat end scene should walk back through the clues and close every red herring, cozy-style. This report maps each framework onto the game's systems and turns it into checklists that extend the in-house µ/R/C tests rather than replace them.

## Three scales, one rule: each unit turns a value and stays half-open

The game's micro/meso/macro split (chip, room, case) matches the nested hierarchies in screenwriting craft. McKee builds story from beat, scene, sequence, act and story. Each level is defined by a value change larger than the one below it, and a scene in which nothing changes is "a non event" ([Shortform on McKee](https://www.shortform.com/blog/robert-mckee-story-structure/)). His practical test is to ask what value is at stake when a scene opens and "Where is this value now?" when it closes ([McKee Story](https://mckeestory.com/do-your-scenes-turn/)). Gulino's sequence approach treats each 10-15-minute sequence as "a short film which mirrors the structure of a complete film" whose conflict is **only partly resolved**, which keeps the audience moving ([The Story Department](https://www.thestorydepartment.com/the-sequence-approach)). Eight sequences plus a finale come close to Malibu's seven rooms plus the final board. That supports the existing rule that local boards "end on the suspicion, not the acquittal." A room is a short film that turns, not one that closes.

Deduction games confirm the same structure as mechanics. Golden Idol's designer required that "each scenario had to serve a macro story beat for the narrative" while standing as a self-contained mystery ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/case-of-the-golden-idol)). MicroMacro: Crime City builds each case from narrow "find X" cards that self-check on the back. Once every card is answered, "you'll have unraveled the mystery," so the macro answer emerges rather than being asked cold ([Opinionated Gamers](https://opinionatedgamers.com/2021/01/20/dale-yu-review-of-micromacro-crime-city/)). Obra Dinn adds a second axis. Pope placed each death "chronologically in time, and order of player discovery," and used the logbook to hold both ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/road-to-the-igf-lucas-pope-s-i-return-of-the-obra-dinn-i-)). That is exactly Cat Detective's split between the evening's true chronology and the player's room order. In detective fiction this is the "double plot": the crime's story is recovered through the investigation's story ([Wikipedia: Clues in detective fiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clues_in_detective_fiction)).

These sources make the in-house framework's R5 test ("rehearses the macro") a structural requirement, not a nice-to-have. A room whose board answers a question the final board never asks breaks Golden Idol's "serve a macro beat" rule. The in-house readout already names three cases. The entrance board never asks for 6:30, which is the entrance's real macro output. The garden board ignores the cases it contains. The kitchen feeds nothing. Swain's scene-and-sequel rhythm adds what should happen *between* rooms. A scene runs goal, conflict, disaster. The sequel that follows runs reaction, dilemma, decision, and the decision sets the next goal ([Wikipedia: Scene and sequel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scene_and_sequel)). In the game, the local solve is the disaster, and the solve-gated "X might have some explaining to do..." toast is the sequel's decision prompt. That is a good reason to keep that toast as the only pointer, as the project already decided.

| Scale | Game unit | Framework anchor | Must-pass (adds to in-house tests) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Micro | chip, one dialogue page | McKee beat; Maass micro-tension; Pelgrane clue/hint/detail | Each page carries a small contradiction, tell or withheld detail; role tag (µ3) uses the GUMSHOE tier (core / hint / detail) |
| Meso | room + local board + confrontation | Gulino sequence; Harmon circle; MicroMacro card; TV act-out | Board answer is, or directly arms, a final answer (R5); last clause is a hook (R8); solve unlocks a turn, not a closure |
| Macro | case + final board + end scene | McKee act/story; Obra Dinn two axes; Christie gathering | Final sentence states the culprit's ledger row plus the Big Lie (C6); end scene walks back through clues and closes every herring (C7) |

Harmon's eight-step Story Circle (You, Need, Go, Search, Find, Take, Return, Change), built for five-minute Channel 101 shorts ([Kindlepreneur](https://kindlepreneur.com/dan-harmon-story-circle/)), works as a room-level beat sheet. The cat enters (You). The board poses its question (Need). He talks to people and inspects objects (Go, Search). Chips unlock (Find). The solve costs a suspect their footing (Take). The confrontation pays it off (Return). The player leaves with a changed belief (Change). If a room has no "Change", the in-house R6 test fails.

## Fair play is a hindsight test, and the macaw case has two soft spots

Knox's and Van Dine's rule sets come down to one contract: the reader gets an equal chance from clues shown on the page, and the culprit is a known character exposed by deduction. Van Dine demands "equal opportunity with the detective" and that the culprit be "a person with whom the reader is familiar and in whom he takes an interest" ([UMSL reprint](https://www.umsl.edu/~gradyf/film/vandinerules.htm)). Knox requires that "the criminal must be mentioned in the early part of the story," that no accident or unaccountable intuition helps the detective, and that twins and doubles appear only if "duly prepared for" ([Wikipedia: Ronald Knox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Knox)). Chandler sets the payoff standard: "The solution, once revealed, must seem to have been inevitable" ([Quotepark](https://quotepark.com/quotes/1747162-raymond-chandler-the-solution-once-revealed-must-seem-to-have-bee/)). A 2025 restatement adds that the culprit must not be "a minor figure who appears briefly and vanishes" ([CrimeReads](https://crimereads.com/a-magicians-contract-between-reader-and-writer-rules-for-delivering-a-whodunit/)). Several period rules can be dropped safely in a cozy: no romance, the corpse requirement, the servant ban. The servant ban is also exactly what makes Rosa work as a herring, because players have absorbed "the butler did it."

The game passes most of the contract by construction. `verify_level.py` enforces ungated sources for every answer (Knox 8, Van Dine R1). Confrontations are "slips, never solutions," which rules out the unmotivated confession (Van Dine R5). The speaker-and-cable trick needs no lecture (Knox 4). "47 ruined takes" is a personal, petty, cozy motive (Van Dine R19). The research flags two soft spots. The first is the culprit's early presence. The deliberate pacing keeps Marsh's *name* out of the first three rooms. That is legitimate, but Knox and Van Dine require the *person* to be met early. The Sound Guy should be a talkable character, or at least a physical presence the player passes, before the library. Otherwise the reveal falls into the "minor figure" failure. The second is planting the mechanism. Under Knox 10's "duly prepared" logic, the player should meet the idea that a speaker can fake a sound in an innocent context before finding the hidden speaker. Derek's spare audio cable and visible film-set sound gear are the natural plants.

| Fair-play gate | Question to ask of a draft | Malibu status |
|---|---|---|
| Culprit introduced early | Can the player interact with the culprit, even unnamed, within the first third of likely play? | Check: Marsh is unnamed until the library by design; confirm the Sound Guy is a present character before room 6 |
| Every link visible | List every inference from opening to "Marsh did it"; does each have a player-readable source? | Enforced for answers; also audit *supporting* links (the 7:00 call on the schedule) |
| Mechanism prepared | Is the trick's mechanism (audio playback) shown innocently before its proof? | Partly (Derek's cable); strengthen |
| No luck or confession | Does any NPC state the conclusion? | Passes (slips only, neutral payoffs) |
| Narrator never ahead | Do toasts or narration interpret evidence? | Passes (Knox 9's dim Watson maps to neutral narration) |
| Inevitability | After the end scene, does replaying the 6:30 registry, the 6:47 photo, the 7:00 manifest and the cable feel like "it was all there"? | Make the end scene show it (see the end-scene section) |
| Fair concealment | Can a player who reads every page, without guessing, reconstruct the 6:30-vs-7:00 half-hour gap? | Yes: explicit inputs, one subtraction; this is the right kind of step |

Rian Johnson's view on surprise and suspense explains why the bedroom-before-living-room funnel is the case's strongest design choice. He sides with Hitchcock that suspense is "a much stronger engine" than surprise, and prefers showing a truth early so the audience asks "Why did that person lie in this way?" ([/Film](https://www.slashfilm.com/570686/rian-johnson-interview-knives-out/)). He also warns against "one big buildup to a cheap surprise at the end" ([SYFY Wire](https://www.syfy.com/syfy-wire/knives-out-director-rian-johnson-explains-how-to-build-a-great-whodunnit-mystery)). The forced route plants "alive at 8" in the bedroom and breaks it at the cage. That is a mid-case reveal that changes the question from *who took it* to *who faked the squawk, and when did the bird really go*. It is the case's Kishotenketsu "Ten," the twist that reframes everything before it without adding conflict ([Book Riot](https://bookriot.com/?p=475083)). The in-house readout's sharpest finding follows from this: the 6:00 departure, the Big Lie's demolition, is never asked on any board. By Johnson's logic the case's best engine pays out only as atmosphere. Asking for it, on the living room board or as a final-board clause, would turn the strongest deduction into a scored one.

## Redundancy belongs on inferences, because keywords already guarantee discovery

Tabletop RPG design offers the most rigorous clue-flow theory, and it changes once the game guarantees delivery. The Alexandrian's Three Clue Rule says: "For any conclusion you want the PCs to make, include at least three clues." The reason is that players "will probably miss the first clue, ignore the second, and misinterpret the third" ([The Alexandrian](https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/1118/roleplaying-games/three-clue-rule)). GUMSHOE solves discovery outright. If a scene holds a core clue and the character uses a relevant ability, "the character will find the clue" ([Wikipedia: Gumshoe System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gumshoe_System)). Cat Detective's `[keyword]` auto-unlock is GUMSHOE delivery: read the page, get the chip. So the remaining failure points are "did I talk to this object" and above all "did I draw the inference." **The Three Clue Rule therefore applies to the interpretation of each board slot, not to finding chips.** WHEN-1 (6:30) is a good model. It rests on the registry, the schedule's 7:00 call and Reyes's registry hunch, which are three independent supports for one subtraction.

Pelgrane's information taxonomy gives the in-house µ3 role tags a principled backbone. **Leads** point to scenes. **Clues** carry means, motive and context, and core clues are guaranteed. **Hints** make sense only in retrospect; "there's no such thing as a core hint" and a hint "opens more questions than it answers." **Details** are background ([Pelgrane](https://pelgranepress.com/2023/03/08/a-taxonomy-of-investigations/)). That matches the project's confrontation rule exactly. Slips like `hall_rumble_no_soup` and `knew_cases_empty` are hints. They are evidence and near-miss decoys, never answers. The Alexandrian also splits *existential* leads (a node exists or matters) from *access* leads (how to get there), and warns that three existential leads "haven't actually fulfilled the Three Clue Rule" ([The Alexandrian](https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/50095/roleplaying-games/running-mysteries-the-two-types-of-leads)). Cross-room character references ("Coco's squeaky wheels," "Marsh stopped complaining") are existential leads, and transfers are access. Any cross-room confrontation gate should have its existential lead planted before the gate can open.

The Alexandrian's node-design rules describe Malibu's map: "each node [has] a minimum of three clues in it and a minimum of three clues pointing to it." The structures include the *funnel* (layers converging on a chokepoint) and the *loop* (enter anywhere) ([Node-Based Scenario Design pt. 5](https://www.thealexandrian.net/creations/misc/node-design/node-design5.html)). Malibu is a funnel at the start (bedroom before living room) and a loop afterwards. The inverted rule, "If the PCs have access to ANY three clues, they will reach at least ONE conclusion," judged only by clues toward *unvisited* nodes ([pt. 2](https://thealexandrian.net/creations/misc/node-design/node-design2.html)), gives a stall test for the free-roam half. For design, Robbins's **revelation list** is the most useful tool: list each conclusion and the clues that point to it, tick it only when players actually *reach* it, and add clues when it stalls ([The Alexandrian](https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/1118/roleplaying-games/three-clue-rule)). Keep structural revelations (board answers) separate from background lore, and run redundancy checks only on the structural list.

| Clue-flow checklist | Pass condition |
|---|---|
| Revelation list per slot | Every local and final slot lists its unlocking source plus 2-3 *supporting* sources that make the right reading reachable, ideally of different media (physical, testimony, document) |
| Chokepoint redundancy | No single room is the sole holder of a final-critical *inference*; if it is, add support elsewhere (Three Clue Rule extension: a chokepoint needs three solutions) |
| Node density | Each room holds at least 3 clues that point outward (existential leads to other rooms or people), and at least 3 sources point to its key fact |
| Lead order | Every cross-room gate has its existential lead planted earlier on the likely path |
| Tier hygiene | Answers = core clues (ungated); slips = hints (never answers); flavor = details, but still yields a chip (project rule) |
| Shortcut audit | Golden Idol: "if players would suddenly bypass many of these steps with some other clue, we knew that we had to remove that clue" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/road-to-igf-2023/-the-case-of-the-golden-idol-i-used-frequent-testing-to-improve-its-mystery-solving)) |
| Contradiction per board | Each local board contains at least one explicit contradiction (testimony vs physical, document vs document); each final WHEN slot has a cross-room one |

The clue literature names contradictions as the engine that turns facts into deductions. Academic accounts separate "rational" clues that "do not fit into the wider picture" from "intuitive" clues about motive and state of mind. They cite York's principle that clues should be individually **non-conclusive**, because proof beyond doubt dispels the mystery ([Wikipedia: Clues in detective fiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clues_in_detective_fiction)). That is the theory behind "one clue = one fact" and "board sentences pose the tension." A chip proves nothing alone. A board is where two chips collide.

## Misdirection works through attention, and every herring owes the case a true clue

Fair concealment hides by attention, never by omission. Three methods recur. **Concealment** hides the key clue among more memorable objects. **Misdirection** follows a clue with something more exciting. **Delay** gives a clue early and attaches its meaning late ([CrimeReads](https://crimereads.com/a-magicians-contract-between-reader-and-writer-rules-for-delivering-a-whodunit/)). The Hopes' study of Christie catalogues "Sawing the clue in half" (two halves of one clue placed far apart), "Plenty of Nothing" (the valueless thing that is priceless) and clue clusters ([Cross-Examining Crime](https://crossexaminingcrime.com/2024/01/06/agatha-christie-plots-clues-and-misdirections-thirty-three-ways-the-queen-of-crime-deceives-us-2023-by-sally-and-tony-hope/)). Each technique already has a home in the game. The registry and manifest chaff entries ("Linen return - 7:10 PM") are concealment by list, and they work only if the key entry is formatted exactly like the noise. The event/time chip split is a textbook sawn-in-half clue: the source states the pairing once, and the player re-pairs it on the board. The cable at the cage, surrounded by the louder glove, polish cloth and broken latch, is misdirection by excitement. Wexler's note that Marsh "stopped complaining" is Plenty of Nothing, an absence clue that is fair and elegant.

Practitioner sources agree on what a red herring is: a real character with a real secret and an honest explanation, revealed later. Calvin advises giving "every one of your significant characters a secret," resolving each herring through confession, motive or alibi, and tracking them in a spreadsheet ([Writer's Digest](https://writersdigest.com/write-better-fiction/planting-clues-red-herrings-that-fool-but-dont-frustrate-your-readers)). Story Grid adds that each herring should "play off" the real answer and pay off with a satisfying reason for the misleading behavior ([Story Grid](https://storygrid.com/red-herrings/)). Van Dine's R2 limits deception to what the criminal does to the detective ([UMSL](https://www.umsl.edu/~gradyf/film/vandinerules.htm)), so herrings must come from in-world behavior. Basil is the model. His secret (he broke the latch to free the bird) explains his guilt, and the truth he confesses hands over a real clue: he witnessed the snatch. Derek is honestly wrong rather than lying, which is fair because Marsh's speaker fooled him too.

The herring checklist below plugs straight into the in-house MMO ledger (0a). A herring has two of three cells filled plus one findable break, and the break is that room's confrontation payoff.

| Herring test | Basil | Rosa | Derek | Chip / Wexler |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| (a) Secret | Broke the latch to free Rudebeak | Motive note, cart in the wing at 7:00, polish cloth at the cage | Ultimatum and spare audio cable | PR panic; delight in drama |
| (b) Why hide it | Guilt, fear | Servant suspicion | Marriage | Comedy |
| (c) Clue that incriminates | Glove, shears match latch | Note, cart, keys, feathers | Headphone box, cable | Light |
| (d) Clue that clears/explains | Garden-solve confession: never opened the door | `cable_grease`: second rumble was equipment | Squawk heard through headphones (sincere) | One line is enough |
| (e) Truth contributes a real clue | Yes: witnessed the snatch | Yes: one cart vs two rumbles | Yes: the planted lie | Optional |
| (f) Resolved on screen by the end | Needed | **Needed: cozy endings "clarify all parts"** | Needed | Optional button |

Row (f) is where the cozy contract is stricter than general mystery advice. Calvin says an immaterial herring "may need no explanation," but cozy guides say the ending "must clarify all parts of the mystery" and that "justice always prevails" ([Writers Write](https://www.writerswrite.co.za/?p=1265040)). Johnson names "a pleasing moral certitude" as a core genre pleasure ([/Film](https://www.slashfilm.com/570686/rian-johnson-interview-knives-out/)). For the main cast, clarify: Basil forgiven, Rosa cleared, Derek's certainty gently punctured. Chip and Wexler can get a single comic line. Two cautions temper all of this. The Alexandrian calls red herrings "overrated" because players invent their own suspects ([Three Clue Rule](https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/1118/roleplaying-games/three-clue-rule)). And Golden Idol keeps evidence to what is "necessary to the solution" or deliberate misdirection ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/case-of-the-golden-idol)). Malibu already has five herrings, so it does not need more. New content should deepen the existing ones rather than add a sixth suspect.

## The case already has a beat sheet; it is missing its 75% low

Western screen frameworks share one skeleton: a catalyst at about 10%, commitment at 20-25%, a midpoint false victory or defeat at 50%, "All Is Lost" at 75%, then finale and a final image that mirrors the opening ([Reedsy: Save the Cat](https://reedsy.com/blog/guide/story-structure/save-the-cat-beat-sheet/)). Field himself stresses the form over exact page numbers ([sydfield.com](https://sydfield.com/guest-blog/utilizing-syd-fields-screenwriting-paradigm-to-understand-script-timeline-structureby-natalia-lazarus/)). The whodunit version is visible in a Murder, She Wrote breakdown: the body at Plot Point 1, alibis and contradictions in Act 2, a **false confession at the midpoint** that Jessica rejects, then a blackout and stolen book as Plot Point 2. The final twist is that the rejected suspect did it after all, followed by a comic button ([DIY MFA](https://diymfa.com/?p=47467)). Jane Espenson adds TV discipline. The Act 1 break must define the problem, "certainly not any later." In five acts the all-is-lost turn sits at the Act 4 out, and "the new act is made out of the cloth in the middle" ([Jane Espenson](https://janeespenson.com/082007/the-book-of-acts)). Act-outs are where reveals land ([GL Coverage](https://glcoverage.com/2025/09/18/writing-act-breaks-for-tv)).

| Beat (Snyder %) | Malibu equivalent | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Opening image / set-up (1-10%) | Poster + one-page typewriter intro | Present; a TV teaser would show the crime: consider one line of the 8 PM squawk and empty cage |
| Catalyst (10%) | Reyes at the entrance, macaw missing | Present |
| Break into two (~25%) | Gated door to the living room, crossed carrying "alive at 8" | Present, and literal |
| B story (~25%) | Basil's "cages are prisons" / freedom vs captivity | Present but could carry the theme more openly |
| Midpoint (50%) | Cage room: the squawk was fake | Present; strongest beat |
| Bad guys close in (50-75%) | Kitchen, garden, library: herrings tighten | Present |
| All is lost (~75%) | **Missing.** Candidate: the leading theory (Basil) seems confirmed, then the garden-solve confession undercuts it | Add |
| Break into three (80%) | Pool area: 6:47 photo + schedule's 7:00 call | Present |
| Finale | Final board (after the confrontation lock) | Present |
| Final image | End-scene beat 5 mirroring the poster | Check the mirror |

Because rooms after the living room can be visited in any order, the 75% low cannot be pinned to a room. It has to be tied to state. Basil's `requiresSolve` confession is the natural candidate because it is solve-gated. To make it work as the low, the garden board should commit the player to Basil (by ending on his suspicion), and the confession should deliberately feel like it *almost* closes the case before the shed testimony pushes on. That is the Murder, She Wrote double bluff turned around: a confession that turns out to be about the wrong crime.

Two procedural models shape the confrontation system. Columbo is the template for solve-gated topics. Suspects underestimate the detective, "his persistent friendly questioning unsettles them into self-incrimination," and the signature "just one more thing" is a return visit ([Wikipedia: Columbo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbo)). A cat who leaves and comes back is in character, and Marsh blurting "the cases are EMPTY" is a Columbo slip. Chronicles of Crime's core verb, "ask person X about card Y" ([Board Game Quest](https://boardgamequest.com/chronicles-of-crime-review)), is the fuller version of `requiresClue` topics. A few more evidence-reaction topics per character would make interrogation feel systemic without a new system. Ace Attorney's history explains why the player must make the move. Takumi's real-time prototype failed because it was "very easy to read past the contradiction." Blocking progress until the player presents it "achieved the sense that you're solving the case with your own reasoning" ([Nintendo Everything](https://nintendoeverything.com/ace-attorney-creator-on-initial-prototype-and-nearly-suspending-development-maya-and-mias-origins-more/)). The local solve is the presented proof. Takumi's warning that reversals become predictable, so "you also have to have characters who don't change" ([Gyakuten Saiban Library](https://gyakutensaibanlibrary.blogspot.com/2021/07/a-long-interview-with-developers-core.html)), argues for keeping Reyes and Petra steady while others flip.

Scene craft gives the per-interaction rule. Every conversation should turn a value: trust (Reyes from dismissive to cooperating), certainty (Derek from sure to doubting), suspicion (Rosa from clear to suspect). Vary the direction, and avoid three same-direction turns in a row ([Jennifer Ellis](https://jennifer-ellis.squarespace.com/blog/2016/3/10/0lh89i31y69nzn0jcsk3arrv6kao62)). Intros can open mid-action (Vivienne "mid-affirmation"), and topic pages should end on the line that carries the slip. That fits the 2-3-sentences-per-page rule and the newline-before-quote convention.

## Feedback granularity is the knob between frustration and brute force

Every deduction game studied treats solution checking as a design problem in its own right, and the evidence points to a different setting for the final board. Golden Idol iterated in public. Per-slot checking frustrated players because "there were so many places they might be wrong." Whole-board right/wrong frustrated them too. The shipped "two or fewer slots are incorrect" indicator rewarded closeness but "reduced the difficulty and length of the game." The team avoided per-slot validation because "players will optimize the fun out of a game" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/case-of-the-golden-idol)). Obra Dinn confirms fates only in batches of three, "to deter guesswork" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Return_of_the_Obra_Dinn)), though players still report bruteforcing some fates ([dukope.com devlog](https://dukope.com/devlogs/obra-dinn/tig-37/)). Duck Detective, the closest tonal analogue (cozy, isometric, animal detective, Golden Idol-style templates), reports an approximate wrong-count and offers an exact highlight only in Story Mode. Reviewers split over whether its deductions felt like trial and error ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duck_Detective:_The_Secret_Salami)). EXIT ships answer cards specifically so players cannot brute-force its code wheel ([Room Escape Artist](https://roomescapeartist.com/2017/07/06/exit-the-game-the-abandoned-cabin-the-secret-lab-the-pharaohs-tomb-review/)).

Set against these, "Incorrect logic - 2/3 fit" on a three-slot local board is a reasonable teaching signal. An exact "N/7" on the final board is closer to per-slot validation than it looks: swap one chip, resubmit, and watch the count. The research does not settle the right setting. No source reports completion or brute-force rates, and Golden Idol shows that coarse feedback costs difficulty too. Still, the direction is clear. The options are, in increasing coarseness: keep exact counts locally but report the final board only when two or fewer slots are wrong (Golden Idol); report per category group (all WHEN slots right or not, as Obra Dinn batches); or add gentle friction such as an in-fiction cooldown line after repeated wrong submits. An optional accessible mode that highlights wrong slots, like Duck Detective's Story Mode, serves the cozy audience without lowering the default challenge. Golden Idol's warning gives a playtest metric: "if a player understands the main mystery immediately and then is stuck for thirty minutes on some technicality" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/road-to-igf-2023/-the-case-of-the-golden-idol-i-used-frequent-testing-to-improve-its-mystery-solving)), the board's wording is the problem, not the mystery.

Boxed games add three lessons about stalls and "done" states. Hunt A Killer's first season is the cautionary case: "we never knew how much we had actually solved nor what we were still missing," so every component seemed to need research. In-character "Inspector's Notes" hints had to be retrofitted mid-season ([Room Escape Artist](https://roomescapeartist.com/2017/11/27/hunt-killer-season-1-review/)). That validates the playtest-3 HUD cues: the SOLVE pulse, room counters, "M/N confronted". EXIT's three-card ladder (nudge, then heavier push, then solution), scored by hints used ([Meeple Mountain](https://www.meeplemountain.com/reviews/exit-the-game-mega-review-puzzling-out)), is the model for a missing feature. An in-fiction ladder ("Dikkie's whiskers twitch...") could (1) name a room or character with unheard topics, (2) name the slot category to reconsider, and (3) fill one slot. That is the Alexandrian's proactive "guy with a gun walks through the door" fallback for a stalled player ([The Alexandrian](https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/1118/roleplaying-games/three-clue-rule)). On scoring, Detective grades on a scale out of 40 where "there isn't necessarily a win or a lose scenario" ([Zatu](https://zatu.com/blogs/reviews/detective-review)). Suspects lets the score pick the ending ([GamingTrend](https://gamingtrend.com/reviews/suspects-review-great-mystery-adventures-for-the-whole-group)). SHCD's 5-points-per-extra-lead penalty draws complaints that the game is "too dependent on reading the author's mind" ([The Stack](https://www.wurb.com/stack/?p=6720)). A cozy game should never penalize revisits, which the timeline mechanic requires. At most, a first-try or few-wrong-submits result might vary a closing line.

| Feedback and stall checklist | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| Local boards (3-5 slots) | Keep exact "N/M fit"; it teaches |
| Final board (7 slots) | Coarsen: threshold ("close" at 2 or fewer wrong) or per-category batch; never name which slot |
| Accessibility | Optional mode that highlights wrong slots (Duck Detective Story Mode) |
| Stall fallback | Opt-in three-tier in-fiction hint ladder; no score penalty, or only an epilogue-line variation |
| Done signals | Keep SOLVE pulse, counters, confrontation lock (validated by Hunt A Killer's failure) |
| Scoring | Scale, never fail; never penalize revisits (SHCD lesson) |
| Single-option slots | Keep the decoy rule; Duck Detective shows trivially fillable slots make the rare real deduction feel "jarring" |

## The end scene should be a gathering, a walk-back and a button

Mystery denouements do three jobs. They confirm the solution, either to everyone at once or privately. They dispatch the culprit. They close character arcs with a lighter button. Christie used the full drawing-room gathering in only 22 of 66 novels, and moved *Styles*' solution there from a witness box after her publisher found it unconvincing ([CrimeReads](https://crimereads.com/agatha-christie-best-denouements-kemper-donovan/)). Poirot's method "casts suspicion on other suspects before finally nailing the culprit" ([New Savanna](https://new-savanna.blogspot.com/2012/05/hercule-poirot-reveal-and-bleg.html?m=1)). Johnson calls the explanation-with-flashbacks "like candy." He also argues that "the really satisfying payoff at the end is not the reveal of whodunit; it comes after that" ([No Film School](https://nofilmschool.com/rian-johnson-knives-out-screenplay)). Boxed games show the cost of skipping this. MicroMacro is criticized for having no big finale ([Meeple Mountain](https://www.meeplemountain.com/reviews/micromacro-crime-city/)), while Hunt A Killer's box-by-box epilogue recap was singled out for praise ([Room Escape Artist](https://roomescapeartist.com/2017/11/27/hunt-killer-season-1-review/)). Takumi adds that players should feel "they solved it themselves, but at the same time also surprise them at the end" ([Gyakuten Saiban Library](https://gyakutensaibanlibrary.blogspot.com/2021/07/a-long-interview-with-developers-core.html)).

In this framing the final board is the player's own gathering and Challenge to the Reader. The confrontation lock plays the role of Ellery Queen's declaration that the reader now holds every clue ([Aunt Agatha's](https://auntagathas.com/aa/?p=7014)). The five-beat end scene then has a clear job split. It should spend its weight on resolution and one new emotional beat, not on a bare confession card.

| Beat | Job | Source pattern | Malibu content |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Gathering; dismiss herrings in turn | Poirot casts suspicion before nailing the culprit | Basil (the latch, forgiven), Rosa (the cart, cleared), Derek (the squawk fooled him) |
| 2 | Walk-back of the evidence chain | Johnson's "candy"; Hunt A Killer recap; Chandler inevitability | 6:30 registry, 6:47 photo, 7:00 manifest, cable to speaker, 47 takes, in chronological order |
| 3 | Culprit dispatched, proportionate | Cozy moral certitude; petty motive means gentle comeuppance | Reyes and the Sound Guy; comic, not grim |
| 4 | New emotional payoff the player didn't deduce | Takumi's final surprise; Johnson's "after the reveal" | Rudebeak reveal (`ShowRudebeak`) |
| 5 | Button mirroring the opening image | Save the Cat final image; Murder, She Wrote comic button | Echo the poster or the intro's first line; Basil's freedom theme lands |

Beat 2 is the one most likely to be missing or thin, and it is where the inevitability test is either won or lost. If the current beats jump from verdict to the bird, add the walk-back, even as a fast card sequence.

## Conclusion

The frameworks surveyed here point less to new content than to re-aiming existing content. Malibu's micro layer and endgame are sound. Its problems are routing problems. Room boards ask questions the final board never asks, and the case's best deduction (the bird left near 6:00, so the 8 PM squawk was staged) is never scored. The cheapest high-value change is to rewrite the entrance, garden and kitchen boards so each rehearses a final-board answer or arms a near-miss (6:30, the cases, one cart versus two rumbles), and to ask for the 6:00 departure somewhere. That turns the in-house R5/C3 failures into the MicroMacro and Golden Idol pattern, where each local answer is visibly a piece of the macro. Only after that are new chips or herrings worth considering, and the evidence says they mostly aren't.

The transferable insight for future cases is that guaranteed delivery moves design effort from discovery to interpretation. Because `[keywords]` make finding chips trivial, every tabletop redundancy rule has to be re-aimed at board slots: three supports per inference, a contradiction per board, a revelation list per slot. Feedback granularity then becomes the main difficulty knob. That suggests a tooling direction already sketched in the in-house framework: an author-only `spine` and `supports` tag per clue, which would let `verify_level.py` print each slot's revelation list and flag any final slot with fewer than two independent supports. The open question no source answers is empirical. No published data gives optimal clue counts or brute-force rates for digital deduction boards, so the final-board feedback setting has to come from silent playtests, run "at least five to seven times" as Golden Idol's team did.
